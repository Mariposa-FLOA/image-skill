# Composition Grammars

Choose one grammar after reading the source. These are decision systems, not templates.

## 1. Crop as tension

Use when one source detail is stronger than the whole frame: an eye, profile, hand, vehicle line, garment, sign, architectural edge, or horizon.

- Enlarge the detail until it becomes a field with a readable edge.
- Let the title measure or resist that edge; do not place it as a caption.
- Keep one area calm enough that the crop feels intentional rather than accidental.

Minimum condition: the crop changes what the source means or makes the familiar feel newly physical.

## 2. Type as architecture

Use when a word can create the composition's main mass.

- Give type a structural role: wall, route, threshold, rhythm, or counterweight.
- Place the photographic anchor in a necessary relationship to that mass: inside, behind, against, crossing, or measured beside it.
- Do not use a giant word as empty background decoration.

Minimum condition: removing the type would collapse the layout, and removing the image would make the type's form less meaningful.

## 3. Measured interval

Use when two source elements have a meaningful relationship: two people, a person and a place, a road and water, a car and an empty horizon, a product and its shadow.

- Build the composition around the exact gap, not merely the elements.
- Use an axis, baseline, or aligned edge to make the distance felt.
- Keep the field around the interval quiet.

Minimum condition: the gap communicates a recognisable condition such as distance, pairing, waiting, pressure, or release.

## 4. Color territory

Use when a source color can become a decisive world rather than a grade.

- Reduce the image to a clean palette of two to four tones.
- Assign a clear territory to each color: photo evidence, field, type, or interruption.
- Prevent color from becoming a generic background wash; it must alter hierarchy.

Minimum condition: changing the territories would change the message and balance of the piece.

## 5. Grid interruption

Use when an orderly system can make one source gesture, object, or crop feel alive.

- Establish a simple grid, column, line, or repeated unit first.
- Let one source-derived element interrupt it precisely.
- Preserve enough order for the interruption to be readable.

Minimum condition: the exception has a source-specific reason rather than random decoration.

## 6. Figure-ground reversal

Use when silhouette, reflection, window, hairline, vehicle contour, road, building aperture, or shadow offers a strong boundary.

- Make the anchor and surrounding field compete for attention through value or color inversion.
- Treat negative space as a real visual form.
- Keep the contour clean; no noisy masking.

Minimum condition: both the positive and negative shapes remain meaningful when viewed separately.

## 7. Controlled sequence

Use when one scene can be read as time, repetition, or multiple views without turning into a scrapbook.

- Limit to two to five fragments, ordered by one rule: scale, time, direction, value, or line.
- Reserve one fragment as the primary anchor.
- Make typography participate in the sequence's pace.

Minimum condition: every fragment changes the reading; remove decorative repetitions.

## 8. Object-led geometry

Use when a source object already carries a visual structure: a vehicle, camera, glass, frame, window, lamp, garment, road sign, chair, or architectural element.

- Let the object's real contour set the grid, crop, or title path.
- Preserve its construction and recognisability.
- Do not turn it into an unrelated symbolic prop.

Minimum condition: the layout could not be made from a generic rectangle in place of the object.

## Selection check

After selecting a grammar, answer privately:

1. Does the source make this choice specific?
2. Can the design be understood before its copy is read?
3. Is there one clear eye path?
4. Can one element be removed without loss? If yes, remove it.
