# Glitchy Glam Editorial Visual

## Mission

Create a polished editorial image with one intentional visual disruption. The subject should feel desirable, clear, and art-directed first; the “glitch” should arrive as a precise fracture in symmetry, colour, surface, crop, or image logic.

The visual proposition is:

> **beauty with one controlled wrongness**

This is a contemporary image-making system for portraits, fashion, beauty, products, objects, and social campaign visuals. It is not a software-glitch filter. The fracture must be designed around the source and must make the image more memorable without destroying the subject.

Use the host's available image-generation, compositing, vector, layout, or local editing tools. Do not assume a particular provider or paid generation route. If the host can only return a prompt, return a production-ready prompt and say no image file was rendered.

## What this Skill owns

Use it for:

- avant-garde beauty and fashion editorials;
- portraits with deliberate asymmetry, dual-tone styling, or a controlled digital/analogue collision;
- glossy product or object images with one unexpected surface or crop interruption;
- social covers and campaign stills that need a contemporary, high-contrast visual hook;
- trend-aware image experiments that must remain original and source-specific.

Do not use it as the primary Skill for:

- a generic glitch overlay or VHS filter;
- a full cyberpunk scene, neon city, or sci-fi world;
- Y2K sticker/cutout collage when the period collage grammar is the main request;
- physical glass, mirror, prism, or water behaviour when believable optics are the main request—use `optical-refraction-visual`;
- a broad FLOA Mariposa brand visual when the brand system, rather than this fracture, is the main request—use `floa-mariposa-visual-system`;
- a page-flip video or PSD export by itself. Hand those outputs to `page-flip-showcase` or `poster-layered-psd-export` after the image direction is resolved.

## Success definition

A successful image has five simultaneous qualities:

1. The person, product, object, or scene remains recognisable and desirable.
2. One fracture is visible within one second at thumbnail size.
3. The fracture changes the composition or reading, not just the surface texture.
4. Colour, light, type, and material all support the same editorial idea.
5. The image feels current without looking like a copied Pinterest trend, stock filter, or generic AI fashion image.

If removing the fracture leaves exactly the same image, the fracture is decorative and must be redesigned.

## Preflight: define the beauty fracture

Before rendering, write a compact internal brief:

- **source anchor:** the face, garment, hand, product, silhouette, object edge, or environmental detail that must survive;
- **glamour surface:** the quality that makes the source desirable—skin clarity, fabric sheen, product finish, colour, light, scale, or pose;
- **fracture:** one intentional break in symmetry, alignment, surface, crop, or colour;
- **editorial frame:** the main field, crop, axis, and quiet zone;
- **palette roles:** base, highlight, signal, and neutral;
- **type role:** none, small label, or structural headline;
- **layer plan:** background, source anchor, fracture, colour/graphic plane, type, and finish when editable output is requested.

Do not begin with the word “glitch.” Begin with what is beautiful and what precise relationship will be interrupted.

## Source-first route gate

Use this Skill only after reading the source's scene, shot scale, people, objects, light, and strongest edge. The fracture must be chosen from what the image gives you:

- close portrait or beauty detail → deliberate asymmetry, skin/colour echo, or a restrained crop fracture;
- medium fashion portrait → garment, accessory, pose, or shadow-based mismatch;
- full-body or wide scene → one spatial or digital/physical interruption that follows the environment's axis;
- product/object close-up → gloss interruption, material mismatch, or contour echo tied to the product shape;
- group scene or busy composition → avoid face-level glitch; use one group-level crop, colour field, or environmental fracture;
- character sheet → use it for identity only, never copy its grid, labels, or moodboard layout.

If the source is better served by a cinematic, optical, collage, symbolic, or geometric route, hand off instead of forcing Glitchy Glam. The trend name is a possible direction, not the decision.

## Build protocol: isolate the source before adding the fracture

### 1. Segment according to the shot

Read the scene and shot scale first, then isolate only the regions the chosen fracture needs:

- **close portrait:** protect eyes, nose, lips, freckles, hairline, and face contour; use a cheek/ear/hair/accessory region for asymmetry or a small colour echo;
- **medium fashion shot:** separate face, garment, hands, accessory, and background plane; let the fracture follow a shoulder, sleeve, hem, or shadow;
- **full-body or wide scene:** preserve the ground, architecture, horizon, and cast shadow; use one environmental crop, colour plane, or offset shadow rather than a face-level glitch;
- **product/object:** isolate the product silhouette, label, functional parts, and contact shadow; apply gloss, colour, or offset only to a meaningful surface or contour;
- **group scene:** protect each face and relationship; use one group-level crop, colour field, or environmental interruption, not multiple individual face effects.

Use clean masks or transparent layers where available. Never let the fracture destroy hair strands, fingers, facial landmarks, product lettering, or the contact between an object and its environment. A character sheet supplies identity evidence; segment the person from the sheet only if the host can preserve the features, and never copy the sheet's grid or labels into the final image.

### 2. Establish the clean base

Compose the scene, subject scale, camera crop, key light, and quiet zone before styling. The base image must already look like a deliberate fashion, beauty, or product editorial without glitch effects. If the base is weak, changing the fracture will only hide the problem.

### 3. Add colour selectively

Choose a base value, one glamour highlight, one signal colour, and a quiet neutral. Apply colour with masks, planes, reflected light, or selected styling regions. For example, a cool blue can belong to one garment edge or shadow while a warm red belongs to one highlight; do not tint the whole image or split every edge into RGB. Preserve natural skin and product material unless the brief explicitly asks for a transformation.

### 4. Add the fracture once

Use one mechanism—chromatic echo, deliberate asymmetry, crop fracture, gloss interruption, digital/physical mismatch, or type-body collision. Define its exact region, edge, opacity, and visual purpose. Add at most one small supporting cue. The fracture must change the reading of the clean base; otherwise remove it.

### 5. Add type only after the image works

Use no text by default for a beauty test. When text is requested, use exact copy, a clear hierarchy, and a real relationship to the crop, contour, or empty field. Set text in a live local layer or SVG when it needs to be correct or editable. Never use random pseudo-editorial microcopy to make the image feel fashionable.

### 6. Keep editable layers when requested

Plan these real roles before rendering:

```text
background / scene field
source anchor / subject cutout
glamour light / cast shadow
fracture / selected offset or surface
colour plane / accent styling
type / approved lockup
texture / finishing detail
```

Use `poster-layered-psd-export` for PSD output. Mark text as raster unless the host created a native Photoshop type layer. Do not turn a flattened image into a fake multi-layer file.

## Choose one fracture mechanism

Select one primary mechanism and at most one small supporting cue:

### Chromatic echo

Shift a controlled edge or duplicate contour into two colour echoes. Keep the original anchor readable; use the echo to suggest motion, signal, or an unstable registration. Do not displace the whole image into unreadable RGB noise.

### Deliberate asymmetry

Create a purposeful mismatch between two sides, such as eye makeup, earrings, colour blocks, gloves, nails, lighting temperature, or styling detail. Preserve facial identity and body logic. The mismatch must feel authored, not like an anatomy error.

### Crop fracture

Interrupt the image with a precise offset crop, split plane, shifted frame, or repeated detail. The two parts must share a measurable edge or alignment. Do not cut randomly through eyes, hands, product marks, or essential text.

### Gloss interruption

Use one lacquer, chrome, wet, translucent, or reflective surface to interrupt an otherwise controlled image. The surface must have a visible boundary and a reason to be there. Do not turn it into a generic glass filter or a pile of specular effects.

### Digital/physical mismatch

Place a clean digital signal, scan field, print fragment, registration error, or interface-like block against a tactile physical surface. Keep the two worlds connected by one edge, colour, or motion relationship. Do not fill the whole image with scanlines, pixels, or fake UI.

### Type-body collision

Let a short headline or product name share a contour, mask, crop, or plane with the source. Use exact text and keep the collision legible. Do not let type become a random sticker floating over a beauty portrait.

## Composition and visual hierarchy

Build in this order:

1. **field:** a clean neutral, dark, or saturated ground;
2. **anchor:** one large recognisable person, product, or object;
3. **glamour light:** one controlled key light or surface highlight;
4. **fracture:** one dominant offset, asymmetry, crop, surface, or mismatch;
5. **editorial type:** only if it strengthens the image;
6. **finish:** restrained grain, halftone, blur, or registration only where it supports the fracture.

Use a visual weight check:

- anchor and glamour: roughly 50–65%;
- fracture: roughly 15–25%;
- supporting graphic or type: roughly 10–20%;
- quiet space: at least 15% when the source permits.

These are checks, not a template. If the fracture becomes the entire image, it is no longer glamour with a controlled wrongness; it is an effect demo.

Keep the subject slightly off-centre or use a deliberate formal balance. Give the eye one clear path: face to fracture, product to surface break, or silhouette to colour echo.

## Colour and light

Use two to four main colours with explicit roles:

- **base:** black, ivory, cool grey, skin-neutral, or a source-derived field;
- **glamour:** one luminous cream, silver, pearl, or controlled warm highlight;
- **signal:** one electric blue, acid green, hot red, violet, or orange interruption;
- **neutral:** a quiet value that protects legibility.

Cool blue can be a strong contemporary route, but never force blue onto every source. Use high saturation on the fracture or signal only; leave enough neutral or dark value for the subject to breathe.

Light should be clean and intentional. Use one key source and one controlled secondary reflection or colour bounce. Avoid universal neon rim light, orange-teal grading, crushed blacks, plastic skin, or gloss over every surface.

## Typography and copy

- Add type only when the brief asks for it or when it completes the editorial composition.
- Use no more than two type families and three size levels by default.
- Keep headlines short; make the text share an edge, axis, mask, or crop with the image.
- Preserve supplied product names and copy exactly. If the product is `FLOA Mariposa`, keep that exact name and do not split it into unrelated labels.
- Do not invent claims, dates, customers, awards, specifications, fake campaign codes, or pseudo-editorial credits.
- For dense or exact copy, use a local text/SVG/layout pass rather than relying on image-model lettering.

## Source and identity protection

- Preserve face geometry, eye spacing, nose, lips, hair, skin tone, expression, body proportions, hands, clothing, and accessories.
- For products, preserve silhouette, material, label, packaging geometry, and functional parts.
- An asymmetry mechanism may alter styling or graphic treatment, but never invent a second face, extra limb, duplicate product, or broken joint.
- Keep important source text readable or contain it in a protected image area.
- Do not use a fracture to hide a weak or incorrectly generated subject.

## Workflow

### 1. Classify the subject

Choose portrait/beauty, fashion, product/object, or place/scene. Identify the source anchor and what makes it attractive before choosing the fracture.

### 2. Choose the visual route

Select one fracture mechanism and one editorial frame. Decide whether the result is clean digital, glossy studio, print-digital hybrid, or tactile editorial. Keep the route specific to the source.

### 3. Make a wordless base

Render or compose the clean anchor and glamour light first. At thumbnail size, the subject should already read as intentional and desirable without the fracture or text.

### 4. Add the fracture once

Apply the chosen mechanism to one meaningful area. Ask what changes in the viewer's reading: motion, identity, distance, surface, attitude, or scale. If nothing changes, replace the mechanism rather than increasing intensity.

### 5. Add type and finish

Only after the image works without text, add a short exact lockup or headline. Use finish details to reinforce the fracture, not to decorate empty space.

### 6. Review and reset

Check the subject, fracture, hierarchy, palette, copy, and originality. If the user says “太普通”“只是加了滤镜”“像赛博朋克”“不够高级” or “太乱,” change the fracture mechanism, crop/scale relationship, palette/value family, and type behaviour before adding more effects.

## Anti-patterns

Reject or rebuild when the result:

- is a normal beauty portrait with RGB offset applied to the whole frame;
- uses random scanlines, VHS bars, pixels, chromatic noise, or digital scratches as the main idea;
- uses five or more simultaneous glitches, mirrors, splits, or reflections;
- turns asymmetry into an incorrect face, duplicated eye, broken hand, or warped product;
- applies neon grid, cyberpunk city, chrome text, and hologram glow without source-specific reason;
- copies the exact look, layout, styling, or signature prop of a supplied reference;
- makes the subject less attractive without gaining a clear visual proposition;
- fills every region with type, stickers, UI blocks, or decorative microcopy;
- uses a trend label as a substitute for a visual thesis.

When resetting, keep the source anchor and exact copy, then change at least three structural decisions: fracture mechanism, crop/scale, palette/value, image-to-type relation, or degree of surface treatment.

## Quality gate

Before delivery, confirm:

1. The source anchor is recognisable at full size and thumbnail size.
2. There is exactly one primary fracture and no more than one supporting cue.
3. The fracture changes an image relationship rather than adding noise.
4. The subject remains desirable, physically coherent, and source-specific.
5. Palette roles and light direction are clear.
6. The quiet zone and eye path are intentional.
7. Type is exact, short, and structurally integrated when present.
8. The image does not collapse into generic cyberpunk, Y2K, VHS, glass, or beauty-filter styling.
9. No copied layout, signature visual device, unsupported claim, or invented identity appears.
10. Any PSD, interactive, or video output is described only after the host actually creates and verifies it.

If checks 1–4 fail, rebuild the image before adding texture or type.

## Editable Photoshop branch

When the user wants a PSD, plan the layer stack before rendering and keep the real independent roles as aligned full-canvas assets: field, source anchor, glamour light, fracture, colour plane, type, texture, and shadow. Use `poster-layered-psd-export` to write and validate the PSD. Mark text as raster unless the host created a native Photoshop type layer. A flattened image cannot be honestly separated into semantic layers afterward.
