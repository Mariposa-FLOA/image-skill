# Optical Laws

Choose one primary optical law. Treat these as material decisions, not effects.

## 1. Curved lens compression

Use a lens, convex glass, or transparent curved vessel to magnify or compress a source feature.

- Best for eyes, profiles, watches, cars, product details, flowers, and signage.
- Let the distortion become stronger toward the curved edge.
- Keep the surrounding world calm enough to reveal the change in scale.

Use only when the magnified detail is specific to the source.

## 2. Waterline displacement

Split a scene at a water surface, glass of water, wet acrylic, or shallow reflective pool.

- The waterline changes clarity and direction but does not randomly duplicate the anchor.
- Use real surface tension, ripples, and reflection only as needed.
- Maintain a clear dry/wet spatial logic.

Best for portraits, hands, objects, coastlines, vehicles, and architecture.

## 3. Mirror seam

Use a crisp or softened mirrored plane to make a controlled second view.

- The seam must sit on a believable plane, object edge, or angled glass.
- Reflection has consistent reverse orientation and matching light.
- Crop can withhold enough information to make the two readings productive.

Do not use a loose double exposure in place of a mirror.

## 4. Translucent color volume

Place the anchor behind or beside colored acrylic, resin, frosted glass, or a gel surface.

- Color shifts according to the material’s thickness and overlap.
- Keep one part of the anchor outside the volume or sharply visible through it.
- Use color as depth, not as a global LUT.

Best for controlled portraits, product stills, and architectural forms.

## 5. Prismatic edge split

Use a small prism or bevel to split only selected edges into restrained spectral shifts.

- Keep the spectrum local at the responsible edge.
- Let the main image remain clean and structurally intact.
- Use only when light direction can plausibly create it.

Do not flood the whole frame with rainbow light.

## 6. Chrome field reflection

Use polished metal, a curved mirror, a vehicle body, or chrome object as a distorted but coherent reflected world.

- Curvature controls how scale and horizon bend.
- Preserve a visual connection between anchor and reflective object.
- Let material highlights describe shape rather than add random shine.

Best for cars, jewelry, technology, furniture, beauty products, and industrial scenes.

## 7. Window-within-window

Use a physical glass pane, display, eyewear, shower door, or architectural window as a second optical frame.

- The pane's edge, reflection, and depth need to be visible.
- Change only what the pane would affect.
- Use external space as a counterpoint to the protected inner view.

Best when the source already has windows, glasses, vehicles, interiors, or storefronts.

## 8. Material threshold

Use a material boundary as a decisive viewpoint: the curved edge of a windshield, a shower door, a pool surface, an elevator mirror, a lens rim, a display case, or a glass table.

- The viewer must feel located on one side of the boundary.
- The boundary changes the anchor and establishes the crop or dominant spatial tension.
- Keep the medium physically tied to the scene; do not introduce it as a gallery prop.

Best when the source already implies transit, privacy, proximity, surveillance, or an inside/outside relationship.

## 9. Optical field

Use light shaped by a material surface—caustics, reflected water, projected glass shadows, or focused sunlight—as the main event rather than showing the material itself.

- Let the light reshape only areas it could reach.
- Build the composition around the moving field and its intersection with the anchor.
- Preserve one untouched area for comparison and visual clarity.

Best for outdoor scenes, faces, textiles, cars, architecture, water, and still life.

## Selection check

Before committing, answer privately:

1. Can the material medium be pointed to in the final image, or proven through the way it casts/refocuses light?
2. Does it obey one plausible optical behavior?
3. What source-specific detail becomes clearer or stranger because of it?
4. Does the medium control the overall crop, silhouette, or spatial tension?
5. If all effects were removed, would the chosen source still make this law meaningful?
