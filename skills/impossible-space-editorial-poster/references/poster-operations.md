# Poster Operations

Use this reference after reading the source and before choosing a spatial route. It converts composition, typography, photography, color, and space into one graphic engine.

## Extract the seed

Choose the source fact with the highest visual leverage:

- a visible word, number, sign, label, or typographic shape;
- a face, silhouette, gesture, product edge, vehicle grille, window, or garment line;
- a repeated rhythm such as lanes, windows, tiles, rails, shadows, or folds;
- a strong contrast in scale, material, light, temperature, or viewpoint.

The seed must remain traceable to the source. Reject a fashionable word, symbol, or shape that could be applied unchanged to unrelated images.

## Choose one primary action

Silently propose at least three actions and select the one that best preserves the source appeal while producing a memorable thumbnail.

### Type as image

Turn one source-derived word or short title into mass, void, rhythm, mask, or perspective. Use counters as windows, let a subject complete a stroke, or let one threshold split the same letter system. Reading the word and seeing the image should happen together.

Reject a large headline placed over unused sky, a centered title over a finished photograph, or decorative letter scattering.

### Overlap and occlusion

Create depth by controlling what sits behind, cuts through, and returns in front. Use two or three deliberate depth states, not a single translucent overlay. Protect faces, hands, product labels, and identifying geometry.

### Crop and scale

Enlarge one source-specific detail until it becomes a field, edge, or point of view. Pair it with a smaller counterweight. The crop must change meaning or tension, not merely increase size.

### Cut and recombine

Split along a source-derived edge, letterform, shadow, frame, or material boundary. Recombine a small number of fragments into a new reading with shared light, thickness, alignment, or rhythm. Reject equal photo boxes and straight before/after seams.

### Double the meaning

Make one element perform two source-supported roles at once: a sign as threshold, a grille as typographic baseline, a window as counterform, or a shadow as room. The second meaning should be discovered, not explained by a tagline.

### Activate the diagonal

Use a source line, gaze, road, shadow, limb, or structural edge to cut through the grid and direct entry, collision, and release. Reject arbitrary rotation and global Dutch-angle styling.

## Integrate the systems

### Image and type

- Prefer source text before inventing copy.
- Use one primary type family and, only when needed, one restrained supporting family.
- Keep supporting copy subordinate and exact.
- Make primary type share at least one physical relationship with the image: edge, mask, counterform, crop, perspective, shadow, reflection, or occlusion.

### Color

- Derive colors from the source, then assign roles rather than applying one LUT.
- Use one dominant field, one counterfield, and one accent.
- Confine a second time, temperature, or reality to the selected graphic structure.
- Keep broad fields clean; grain and distress are off by default.

### Photography

- Preserve the source's desirable identity, product geometry, fashion attitude, era, and material quality.
- Decide whether realism belongs in the subject, one material, or both; simplify secondary fields.
- Use lighting and shadow to explain depth before adding outlines or effects.

### Space

- Select a spatial route only after the poster action is clear.
- When the graphic engine is loud, make the spatial act quiet and exact.
- When the spatial act is loud, reduce typography to structural support.

## Thumbnail gate

At thumbnail size, answer all four:

- What is the first-read silhouette or word?
- Where do image and type or two planes collide?
- What source-specific fact prevents the design from becoming a template?
- What can be removed without weakening the idea?

If the answer is only title, color grade, local reflection, texture, or explanation, redesign before generation.
