# Spatial Routes

Choose one primary route according to the source. Routes are conceptual systems, not fixed layouts.

## 1. Sectional cutaway

Best for interiors, façades, storefronts, apartments, vehicles, furniture systems, and people whose relationship to rooms matters.

### Visual act

Remove or fold one exterior surface to reveal two to four connected spaces. Preserve wall thickness, floor levels, door logic, furniture scale, and the relationship between inside and outside.

### Strong source cues

- windows, doors, balconies, frames, cabinets, corridors, seating bays;
- repeated rooms or architectural bays;
- a person located near a threshold;
- an object whose hidden interior can carry meaning.

### Composition options

- one dominant vertical section with a smaller exterior counterweight;
- diagonal section revealing stacked rooms;
- one room nested inside a garment, façade, vehicle, or product body;
- a continuous stair or corridor joining incompatible levels.

### Reject

Generic dollhouse views, too many equally important rooms, missing wall thickness, impossible stairs, random furniture, and tiny scenes that require explanatory labels.

## 2. Displaced perspective

Best for roads, plazas, city streets, stairways, long rooms, waterfronts, architectural portraits, and scenes with a strong vanishing direction.

### Visual act

Let two or three coherent perspective systems meet at one source-derived threshold. A road may rise into a wall, a façade may become a floor, or sky may behave as a room plane. Each system remains internally believable.

### Strong source cues

- lane lines, curbs, rails, lamp rows, tiles, windows, columns, shadows;
- one clear horizon or corridor;
- a person or object whose orientation can reveal the shift;
- a repeated rhythm that can bridge perspectives.

### Composition options

- one large folded corner with a small human anchor;
- a floor that turns upward at a doorway or horizon;
- two vanishing systems separated by a clean void;
- a shadow obeying a different plane while retaining one plausible light source.

### Reject

Global fisheye distortion, random Dutch angles, melted buildings, every edge bending, duplicate horizons with no threshold, or perspective errors disguised as surrealism.

## 3. Floating spatial modules

Best for portraits, products, objects, travel scenes, memories, routines, collections, and sources with several distinct but related zones.

### Visual act

Extract two to five source-derived spaces as solid modules and suspend them inside one controlled field. Modules must have thickness, scale hierarchy, light coherence, and a readable relationship.

### Strong source cues

- one person interacting with multiple meaningful places or objects;
- furniture islands, platforms, windows, vehicles, product components;
- clean silhouettes that can survive extraction;
- repetition, choice, distance, or memory as the emotional contradiction.

### Composition options

- one dominant module and two small satellites;
- modules aligned by gaze, shadow, path, cable, stair, or color spill;
- one missing module represented by a shaped void;
- the same intact person appearing at separate stages when time is the concept.

### Reject

Random floating photo cards, equal-sized boxes, decorative drop shadows, unsupported object collections, repeated faces with identity drift, and collage density without spatial meaning.

## Route selection test

Select the route that answers yes most strongly:

- Does the source contain physical structure that makes this route specific?
- Can the transformation be read without explanatory text?
- Can the subject remain intact rather than being deformed?
- Can the route produce one clear far-distance silhouette?
- Would another route weaken the emotional contradiction?

If none passes, do not force impossible space. Use a different visual Skill.
