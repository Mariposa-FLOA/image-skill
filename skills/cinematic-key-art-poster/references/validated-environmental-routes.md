# Validated Environmental Poster Routes

Read this reference only when place, infrastructure, repetition, or a tiny human inside a large environment is the strongest cinematic asset. These routes were forward-tested on real source photographs. They are decision patterns, not reusable layouts.

## Route 1: directional rhythm

Use when a road, railing, lamps, columns, lane marks, vehicles, or repeated architecture already creates movement toward a threshold.

- Convert repeated source elements into a measurable visual rhythm that accelerates, slows, or refuses arrival.
- Preserve one destination or opening; simplify incidental vehicles and signs into scale evidence.
- Let the title follow a real perspective edge only when the surface remains readable and the type does not look painted accidentally onto an object.
- Build the far read from one broad field, one directional wedge, and one small chromatic event.
- Keep the result physically plausible: repetition may be intensified, but every pole, lane, shadow, or railing still needs a believable anchor.

The forward-test image is not bundled in this public release; use the decision logic, not a copied image.

## Route 2: vertical pressure and horizontal escape

Use when trees, poles, towers, cables, walls, or columns dominate a scene and a person or horizon appears small between them.

- Treat unequal verticals as pressure, order, ritual, surveillance, or confinement.
- Preserve one thin horizontal opening as air, sea, distance, or escape.
- Keep the person tiny when scale is the emotional fact; do not enlarge them into a generic character poster.
- A mechanical pole may interrupt natural forms, but do not invent giant eyes, interface graphics, or unsupported threats.
- Vertical typography can echo the structure, but it must remain optically separate from cables, faces, people, and incidental signage.

The forward-test image is not bundled in this public release; use the decision logic, not a copied image.

## Shared clean-master checks

- Compare the new result with both examples and change at least three of: dominant axis, negative-space shape, title zone, color stance, horizon height, human scale, and campaign motif.
- Inspect every repeated thin element for melted joints, floating bases, doubled edges, or impossible perspective.
- Remove or neutralize incidental readable source text unless the user explicitly asks to preserve it.
- Verify exact title and tagline character by character. Widely spaced and vertical titles are especially vulnerable to missing letters.
- Keep broad sky, road, wall, water, and grass planes quiet. Detail belongs to the chosen motif, not everywhere.
- Reject an unchanged location photograph with premium grading. The wordless composition must already express the film question.
