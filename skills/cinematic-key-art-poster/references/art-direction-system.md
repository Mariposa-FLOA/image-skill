# Film Poster Art Direction System

Read this file for every new one-sheet. It prevents the Skill from collapsing into a single prestige-drama template.

## 1. Decide the marketing job

Choose one job before composing:

- **teaser:** create curiosity through one memorable absence, object, crop, or contradiction;
- **primary one-sheet:** communicate the film promise and identity clearly enough for release marketing;
- **character poster:** let one performance, costume, gesture, or identity device dominate;
- **festival or repertory poster:** privilege interpretation, medium, and cultural specificity over star size;
- **platform thumbnail:** prioritize a one-second silhouette and face or title legibility at small scale.

Do not force every request into a primary one-sheet with two large actors.

## 2. Write the art-direction thesis

Write one private sentence of six to twelve words that describes the visual act, not the plot or mood.

Weak: `Two people divided by the sea.`

Useful: `One face survives only inside the other's missing horizon.`

The thesis must determine crop, scale, medium, color, and title behavior. If those decisions could remain unchanged after replacing the thesis, it is not directing the art.

## 3. Select a visual strategy family

Choose one family; do not combine several by default.

### Iconic reduction

One object, body fragment, shape, or mark carries the campaign. People may be absent or tiny. Use when a source-derived detail has strong symbolic potential.

### Disruptive crop

An unusual crop, obstruction, off-frame body, cut face, or impossible close view creates tension. Use when performance or gesture is stronger than environment.

### Environmental field

The world occupies most of the poster and people become evidence inside it. Use when place, architecture, weather, or distance is the story engine.

### Relational geometry

Spacing, overlap, rhythm, gaze, or scale expresses a relationship without defaulting to equal opposing portraits. One person may be tiny, absent, reflected, or represented by a trace.

### Material metaphor

Clothing, paper, glass, water, food, smoke, thread, paint, or another source material physically becomes the story. The material behavior must be specific to the source.

### Typographic image

The title or one letter becomes the image through a source-derived physical behavior. Use only after the concept survives without decorative type.

### Staged tableau

Reconstruct a precise scene with theatrical blocking, prop logic, and authored light. Use when a single frozen action can imply before and after.

### Illustrated interpretation

Translate the film into painting, drawing, collage, print, naive image-making, or another authored medium. Use when photographic realism narrows the emotional range.

## 4. Compose with masses, not objects

Start with three value or color masses. Assign them unequal roles: dominant field, counterweight, and accent. A useful starting ratio is roughly 60/30/10, but depart when the idea requires it.

Choose the axis deliberately:

- off-center vertical tension;
- low or high horizon;
- one diagonal interrupted by a counter-shape;
- circular or radial pull;
- edge-weighted composition with a large active void;
- compressed band or panoramic strip inside the portrait format;
- deliberate frontal symmetry when the story is about ritual, control, doubling, or confinement.

Avoid accidental centered balance. A centered object plus two equal actors is not automatically a concept.

Use negative space as active narrative territory. It may conceal, isolate, delay, or amplify; it is not merely room for a title.

For inset scenes, panoramic strips, and nested frames, decide the spatial relationship before rendering: **separate**, **embedded**, or **occluding**. Separate elements need visible breathing room from the actor. Embedded elements need a believable host surface such as glass, water, shadow, fabric, or architecture. Occluding elements must conceal the body with an intentional shape and narrative reason. Never allow an inset rectangle to drift across a face as an unresolved editing artifact.

## 5. Make actor scale intentional

Choose one hierarchy:

- star-led close crop;
- one lead dominant, others as traces;
- actors small within a world;
- actors represented by clothing, shadow, reflection, handwriting, object, or absence;
- no visible actor when the teaser concept is stronger without one.

Recognizability does not require equal size, full bodies, or centered faces. Preserve likeness wherever a face is shown, but do not show every face by habit.

## 6. Choose a color stance

Do not default to dark navy plus amber skin. Select one source-supported stance:

- high-key ivory or pale mineral field with one dark interruption;
- dense single-hue field with one complementary accent;
- daylight naturalism with unusual chroma hierarchy;
- black and white with one source-derived color event;
- dusty low-chroma earth family with one cold synthetic hue;
- high-chroma graphic field with restrained photographic skin;
- warm field with cool human absence;
- colored shadow and neutral highlights;
- almost colorless world with one saturated costume or object.

Set a chroma budget. One hue dominates, one supports, and one may act as a small event. Avoid distributing color evenly.

## 7. Choose a light behavior

Lighting need not always be low-key cinema lighting. Choose one:

- high-key overcast clarity;
- flat direct flash;
- hard noon shadow;
- single theatrical spotlight;
- projected pattern or reflected color;
- backlit silhouette;
- practical-light pool inside darkness;
- window or doorway cut;
- graphic light with no photographic source when the medium is illustrated.

The light must support the thesis. Do not add rim light to every person or sunset to every landscape.

## 8. Integrate type as a spatial actor

Choose one behavior: frame, obstruction, cut, scale contrast, repetition, edge falloff, physical imprint, label, annotation, or quiet caption. Do not automatically put a tracked uppercase title at the bottom.

Type may be very large or very small, but its scale must create a relationship with the image. Keep exact spelling and avoid fake credits.

## 9. Run the distance and recurrence tests

- **one-second test:** one mass, silhouette, crop, or color event is memorable;
- **three-distance test:** far read, actor/world discovery, close clue;
- **recurrence test:** compare with recent outputs; do not reuse the same axis, actor scale, title zone, dominant palette, and symbol placement;
- **omission test:** identify what was deliberately removed and why;
- **medium test:** explain why this image could not be equally effective as an ordinary photograph;
- **taste test:** remove any element whose only function is to signal generic cinematic prestige.

## International practice distilled

- A strong poster can make the star very small when color and specificity carry recognition.
- A costume, fold, material, or printed artifact can embody the premise more powerfully than a scene montage.
- Complexity should be abandoned when it obscures the film; simplicity can be the radical decision.
- Illustration, naive drawing, collage, typography, and graphic reduction are first-class film-poster media, not fallback styles.
- Every small detail should come from the film world; density without specificity is decoration.
