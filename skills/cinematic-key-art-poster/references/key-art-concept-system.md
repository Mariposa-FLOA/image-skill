# Key Art Concept System

Read this reference for every new movie-poster concept and whenever a previous result feels generic. It governs the idea, campaign motif, image-making method, and marketing read. `poster-system.md` governs route, genre, color, and type after the concept is selected.

## 1. Extract the film promise from the source

Write five private observations before ideation:

- **literal fact:** who or what is visibly present;
- **emotional conflict:** what opposing forces can be read without inventing plot;
- **specific evidence:** at least three unique details in face, gesture, clothing, place, prop, weather, architecture, or text;
- **unanswered question:** what the viewer should wonder;
- **market promise:** what kind of emotional experience the poster offers.

Do not confuse a genre label with a concept. `Prestige drama`, `thriller`, `romance`, and `science fiction` are lenses, not ideas.

## 2. Create three propositions, select one

Each proposition needs a sentence in this form:

> Use **[source-specific motif]** transformed by **[image-making method]** to make the viewer feel **[contradiction]** and ask **[question]**.

The three propositions must differ in image logic, not just title placement:

- one can be **human-led**: face, body, gesture, gaze, omission;
- one can be **world-led**: architecture, weather, horizon, room, landscape;
- one can be **symbol-led**: prop, material, reflection, trace, physical metaphor.

Score each candidate from 0–2 on five criteria: source specificity, one-second recognition, emotional tension, medium potential, and campaign extensibility. Select the highest-scoring candidate. If tied, prefer the one that changes the source image most meaningfully while preserving its truth.

## 3. Choose a campaign motif

A campaign motif is one reproducible visual rule, not a decorative object. It should survive across a one-sheet, teaser, social crop, motion card, and title treatment.

Useful motif behaviors include:

- something divides, binds, erases, floods, stains, repeats, refracts, compresses, grows, folds, burns through, or refuses alignment;
- a source object changes scale or function;
- the environment behaves like the emotional conflict;
- a missing region becomes the story;
- two realities share one physical surface.

The motif must come from the source or a restrained inference. Do not import fashionable symbols unrelated to the image.

## 4. Select the image-making method

The method must embody the motif:

- **art-directed photography:** rebuild pose, lighting, wardrobe, or prop relationship when performance and recognizability lead;
- **sculptural still life:** let one object or material carry the film when faces are secondary;
- **optical construction:** reflection, refraction, projection, shadow, lens, water, glass, or mirror creates the contradiction;
- **surreal compositing:** combine plausible source elements into one impossible but legible image;
- **graphic reduction:** reduce to a memorable silhouette, shape, field, or limited palette when instant recognition matters;
- **illustrated or printed interpretation:** use drawing, painting, screenprint, offset, or tactile mark-making when the world benefits from authored distance;
- **restrained montage:** combine fragments only when their collision creates meaning; never use montage as a substitute for choosing an idea.

Do not default to photoreal compositing. A poster can be more cinematic by being less literal.

## 5. Build the visual architecture

Design from far to near:

1. **three-meter read:** one dominant silhouette or color mass;
2. **one-meter read:** actor, relationship, world, or symbol becomes clear;
3. **handheld read:** title detail, texture, secondary clue, and atmosphere reward attention.

Use scale and omission intentionally. Preserve a clean entry point, a directed eye path, and one zone of unresolved information. Do not distribute interest evenly across the canvas.

For actors, decide whether marketing requires face recognition, body recognition, or deliberate concealment. Preserve likeness where a face is used; do not enlarge every face by habit.

## 6. Make color part of the premise

Choose color from story evidence: location materials, costume, time of day, skin, prop, weather, or motif. Give each major hue a role such as human warmth, institutional cold, memory, contamination, rupture, or distance.

Use local color relationships rather than a global LUT. A successful grade changes the reading order and emotional temperature while retaining believable skin, material detail, and highlight behavior.

### Build a light script

Define the poster's light as a short sequence:

1. where the eye enters through brightness or color contrast;
2. which face, symbol, or threshold receives the first motivated light;
3. how a second reflected or opposing light reveals conflict;
4. where the image falls quiet.

Useful clean atmosphere structures include:

- pearl daylight with one warm reflected edge;
- cool dusk field with a low amber horizon and neutral skin;
- near-monochrome world with one source-derived colored spill;
- dark architectural field cut by one soft light corridor;
- pale overcast environment with deep colored negative fill;
- reflected water light shaping faces and objects from below.

Atmosphere must remain optically clean. Do not simulate mood with grain, smoke particles, bloom everywhere, heavy vignette, or a global color wash.

### Allocate a finish budget

Choose where detail is allowed before adding finish:

- one **primary tactile zone** for the campaign motif;
- optionally one **secondary human or environmental zone**;
- all remaining planes should be quieter, smoother, and lower in micro-contrast.

Grain, halation, bloom, diffusion, scratches, wet gloss, print distress, and sharpening are separate effects. The default master uses none of them except ordinary optical softness and restrained capture sharpness. Activate an analog or distressed effect only when the user explicitly asks for that medium. Never stack effects merely to make a digital image feel cinematic.

For composites, normalize sharpness, grain size, black level, and highlight behavior across components, but do not texture every component equally. Clean edges and coherent light sell the construction better than added grit.

If a clean photographic pass repeatedly invents texture, change the medium rather than repeating negative prompts. Keep identity-bearing areas photographic; convert water, sky, architecture, metal, shadow, and graphic symbols into broad, smooth shapes or controlled gradients. This is a redesign decision, not a denoise effect.

## 7. Design a title identity, not a caption

After the wordless image works, decide how the campaign motif affects type. Possibilities include one interrupted letter, a repeated gap, material embossing, perspective lockup, concealed stroke, optical doubling, or analog degradation. Keep the device singular and legible.

Avoid default luxury serif, widely spaced capitals, centered title over sky, and unrelated condensed sans. If plain typography is the right decision, its placement, scale, rhythm, and contrast must still belong to the image architecture.

## 8. Campaign and rejection tests

Before delivery, run these tests:

- **no-text test:** the film promise survives without title or tagline;
- **swap test:** replacing the source with a similar stock image destroys the concept;
- **thumbnail test:** the motif remains identifiable at feed size;
- **silhouette test:** the major value masses still work in grayscale;
- **detail-budget test:** no more than three regions attract attention through texture alone;
- **clean-plane test:** skin, water, sky, walls, or shadows contain at least one calm tonal field without crawling noise or oversharpening;
- **zero-grain test:** for the clean default, no film grain, sensor noise, stippling, print speckle, or artificial pore texture is visible at 100% inspection;
- **light-source test:** the dominant and supporting light can each be traced to a plausible source or reflective surface;
- **depth-light test:** foreground, subject, and world remain separable through broad value or color behavior without relying on texture;
- **specificity test:** at least three source details remain visible or structurally encoded;
- **question test:** the image opens one deliberate question rather than merely describing a scene;
- **extension test:** the motif can produce at least two campaign variants without inventing a second idea.

Reject generic streaming-poster formulas unless the user explicitly asks for them: split protagonists, centered skyline, stacked floating heads, full-body hero under giant title, orange/teal conflict coding, empty upper sky reserved for copy, and scene stills with cinematic grading.

## Case-derived principles

These principles are distilled from international poster and film-identity practice; use them as decision guidance, never as layouts to copy.

- Research across poster history can widen the available visual languages beyond contemporary streaming templates.
- A story contradiction can be embedded in a single mark, object, or typographic interruption instead of explained with multiple scenes.
- A title identity can encode the film's premise and remain useful across posters, trailers, language versions, and small icons.
- Explore the complete film world—performance, costume, set, material, and theme—before reducing it to one campaign motif.
- Different posters in a campaign may reveal different story facets, but they should share a recognizable visual universe.
- Analog or tactile processing is meaningful only when its production behavior echoes the film's world.
